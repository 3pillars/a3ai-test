This one is different from the posts I usually get you. It is a long form article, it is unusually well sourced, and it is the author correcting his own earlier bearish post about artificial intelligence overbuilding. The title is "Jevon's paradox is lazy analysis, evaluating the risk of AI overbuilding with hard maths." His headline number is that four point five nine trillion dollars of annual revenue would be needed to absorb the coming datacenter buildout, and he puts a fifteen to twenty three percent probability on that failing.

Let me give you what he got right first, because it is a lot.

The SemiAnalysis reading is correct. He previously treated a fifty gigawatt figure as cumulative installed capacity, and he now correctly identifies it as net new additions for twenty twenty seven. I checked the SemiAnalysis datacenter industry model directly. The chart is titled global datacenter capacity net additions, annual, gigawatts of I T capacity added, global excluding China. The numbers are fifteen point six for twenty twenty five, twenty five for twenty twenty six, and fifty one point two for twenty twenty seven. So his cumulative build of ninety one point eight gigawatts by the end of twenty twenty seven is arithmetically correct, and it matches his own chart.

The arithmetic is right too. Half of ninety one point eight is forty five point nine gigawatts of inference capacity. Multiply by a hundred billion dollars per gigawatt and you get four point five nine trillion dollars. That checks out. And his own chart footnote is honest about it. It says the twenty twenty six to twenty twenty seven figures are fair revenue based on inference math, and that actual revenue is expected to be much lower.

The DeepSeek pricing is real. He quotes DeepSeek V four point one Flash, and the published price sheet matches exactly. Half a cent per million cached tokens, fifteen cents uncached, sixty cents output off peak, and double that at peak.

And the best part of the whole argument, the hundred billion dollars per gigawatt figure, is not just DeepSeek marketing. SemiAnalysis's own Vera Rubin modelling puts a hundred and fifty nine point five billion dollars of revenue and a hundred and forty nine point nine billion of profit per all in utility gigawatt, and a hundred and fourteen point nine billion for the strongest G B three hundred configuration. So the order of magnitude is corroborated independently.

Now what is wrong, and there is a lot.

First, he mixes two different meanings of gigawatt. The SemiAnalysis series is I T capacity, meaning compute load. The revenue per gigawatt figures he multiplies by are utility gigawatts, meaning facility power. At a power usage effectiveness of about one point one five, those two differ by roughly thirteen to fifteen percent, and you cannot divide one by the other.

Second, and this is the serious one, he leaves out the installed base. His ninety one point eight gigawatts counts only twenty twenty five onward. Epoch AI's own data, which he cites, puts North American datacenter I T capacity at about thirty six gigawatts at the end of twenty twenty three and about forty eight at the end of twenty twenty four. So the pre twenty twenty five base was already the same order of magnitude as the entire twenty twenty five to twenty twenty seven buildout, and it is not in his denominator. On his own source's basis, global capacity excluding China at the end of twenty twenty seven is plausibly one hundred thirty to one hundred sixty gigawatts. He has quietly modelled only the newest, most efficient, and most price sensitive fleet.

Third, he attributes to SemiAnalysis the claim that there are enough accelerators to fill all that capacity. SemiAnalysis says close to the opposite. Their own warning is that datacenters and power may be the tip of the iceberg, and that memory and logic possibly become the new number one constraint in twenty twenty seven and twenty twenty eight. They never guarantee accelerator availability.

Fourth, his forty five point nine gigawatts does double duty. He uses it as the fifty percent inference capacity, and then he also uses the same forty five point nine gigawatts as the training capacity. That is a hundred percent utilisation scenario with zero slack, which directly contradicts his claim that the hundred billion per gigawatt figure holds even assuming a lot of slack.

Fifth, the lab margin baseline is wrong. He says margins fall from seventy to ninety percent to under twenty or thirty percent. Anthropic's S one filing, which went public on the twenty sixth of October, shows compute and infrastructure at one hundred sixty percent of revenue in twenty twenty five and fifty five percent in the first half of twenty twenty six. That is a gross margin around forty five percent. Adjusted operating margin was nineteen percent in the second quarter. So frontier labs are already at about forty five percent gross, not seventy to ninety.

Sixth, his margin arithmetic does not close. Twenty billion revenue against ten billion cost is a fifty percent gross margin before any operating expense. You cannot also claim net margins under twenty or thirty percent from those same three numbers.

Seventh, the Anthropic spending figure is not disclosed as stated. He says just under sixteen billion dollars on training and research since founding. The S one shows cumulative research and development of about eighteen point eight billion dollars, of which compute and infrastructure is about nineteen point two billion. Right order of magnitude, wrong number, and it is not a disclosed line item.

Eighth, and this is the biggest miss, the rhetorical weight he puts on the sixteen billion is misplaced. The same S one discloses aggregate future minimum payment obligations for compute capacity of about four hundred seventeen billion dollars, with two hundred sixty five point six billion of fixed guaranteed commitments as of June thirtieth twenty twenty six. Roughly one hundred fourteen point five billion committed to Google, one hundred ten billion to Amazon, forty four point nine billion to Microsoft, forty four point six billion to Nscale, forty one point three billion to Lambda, and up to one hundred twenty point five billion to Space X A I. The binding constraint on labs is not what they have spent. It is what they have already contracted on take or pay terms.

Now the numbers that cannot be checked. The thirty eight dollars per G P U hour and a hundred and fifty five trillion tokens per day are DeepSeek's own reported production figures. An independent read of the DeepSeek inference engine notes these are reported revenue, not G P U rental cost, not net profit, and the accounting to reproduce either is not published. So the whole chain from thirty eight dollars per hour down to a hundred billion per gigawatt rests on a single vendor's self reported number. The two hundred thirty tokens per second per user figure sits between two endpoints of the decode curve that cannot legitimately be combined, and the twenty three percent probability of failure is stated with no model behind it.

So the verdict. The direction of the argument is well supported. The magnitude is an artifact. The real overbuild risk in twenty twenty seven sits in the tail. Of the fifty one point two gigawatt twenty twenty seven addition, eleven point nine gigawatts, which is twenty three percent, are only planned, not yet under construction. Those are the ones exposed to cancellation or financing failure. The thirty nine point four gigawatts that are operational or under construction are largely sunk. Underwrite the delta, not the headline.

And here is the part that cuts against his own conclusion, which he leaves out. Epoch AI's data shows the price to reach GPT four level performance on PhD level science questions falling about forty times per year. If token prices keep falling anywhere near that rate, revenue per gigawatt falls even as capacity grows. His own cited source makes his hundred billion per gigawatt anchor weaker, not stronger. And the Epoch number makes the cheerful scenario more likely, that cheap frontier models absorb the capacity instead of failing to.

One more bullish datum his own source contains that he does not mention. The twenty twenty seven buildout is already about one trillion dollars of binding, non cancellable datacenter leases signed year to date. Demand is contracted, not speculative. That undercuts the framing that this is speculative overbuilding.

Finally, the part you should actually carry into your own work. For credit, the risk is take or pay and duration, not whether demand exists. Anthropic's four hundred seventeen billion of minimum compute obligations, two hundred sixty five point six billion fixed, against an eighty point seven billion first half loss and a nineteen percent adjusted operating margin, on a price per token curve that is falling. That is the cleanest expression of the exposure: a lab paying for fixed committed capacity at prices it no longer needs. Watch the same thing in the names you already track, where the equity is falling while credit spreads have not confirmed the stress. This is a fixed obligation story, not a demand story.
