Here's my verified check on the Zephyr post, the DRAM math that rebuts the humanoid robot memory supercycle.

The verdict: the math is correct and it checks out exactly, line by line. This is a clean, well-built debunk, and it is the more rigorous of the two takes on the topic tonight.

What he quotes. He is replying to a post by Wall Street Engine relaying a Counterpoint Research note from October tenth, twenty twenty-six. Counterpoint expects DRAM demand from humanoid robots to grow about twenty times by 2030, from roughly eight thousand three hundred terabits in 2026 to a hundred seventy-two thousand terabits in 2030, and expects memory per robot to more than double, from nineteen gigabytes today to thirty-nine gigabytes by 2030.

What Zephyr does. First, he back-solves the production assumption. If total 2030 demand is a hundred seventy-two thousand terabits and each robot carries thirty-nine gigabytes, that implies roughly five hundred fifty thousand humanoids a year. Second, he converts to bytes. A hundred seventy-two thousand terabits is about twenty-one and a half petabytes. Third, he stress-tests it. Even if annual production were twenty times higher, eleven million units, total demand would be four hundred thirty petabytes, or zero point four three exabytes. Fourth, he compares to total DRAM production of about forty-eight exabytes in 2026. So twenty-one and a half petabytes is roughly zero point zero four percent of 2026 DRAM output. Even the twenty-times case is under one percent.

I re-derived every step. One hundred seventy-two thousand terabits equals twenty-one and a half petabytes, correct. The growth ratio is twenty point seven times, correct. The implied five hundred fifty thousand robot production figure back-solves to thirty-nine point one gigabytes per robot, which matches Counterpoint's own thirty-nine gigabyte per robot figure exactly. That is the strongest part of his post, because it means the five hundred fifty thousand production assumption is Counterpoint's own, not an invention. Twenty-one point five petabytes against forty-eight exabytes is zero point zero four five percent, which rounds to his zero point zero four percent. The twenty-times case, four hundred thirty petabytes, is zero point four three exabytes, or about zero point nine percent of production. Everything checks.

The one soft number is the denominator, the forty-eight exabytes of 2026 DRAM production. I could not pin that exact figure to a primary source, but I did find corroborating scale. TrendForce, citing the Commercial Times, has cloud high-speed memory consumption reaching about three exabytes by 2026, and on equivalent wafer usage AI consuming close to twenty percent of global DRAM supply. If high-speed memory alone is a few exabytes and represents roughly a fifth of the total, then total DRAM output in the tens of exabytes is the right neighborhood, and forty-eight is plausible. If the true denominator were half that, robot demand would be about zero point one percent instead of zero point zero four. Still rounding error. If it were double, the number gets smaller. The order of magnitude, well under one percent, holds either way.

Musk is answering a different and much bigger question, about robots as compute substrate generally, and about superintelligence compute, which is not the same claim. Musk's reply never engages Zephyr's arithmetic, and the "physical substrates" line is Elon-speak rather than an argument.

Here is the sharper point. Freda Duan cites the Musk reply as support for the robotics-driven memory supercycle, without mentioning that the tweet she is citing is Musk dismissing the one analyst who actually quantified the robot memory demand, and she does not engage his numbers. That is the weak link in her post, and it is the reason her take eight does not hold up the way her take one does.
