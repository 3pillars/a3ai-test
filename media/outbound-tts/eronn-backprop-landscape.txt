This is a post on X by a creator whose handle is spelled E R O N N, posted this morning, with a seventeen minute visualization of how neural networks actually train. Let me verify the claims and give you the takeaways.

First the headline claim. Around nineteen eighty two, Geoffrey Hinton, spelled G E O F F R E Y, told David Rumelhart that backpropagation would not work, because gradient descent gets stuck in local minima. Then in twenty twenty four, Hinton shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for exactly that method.

That checks out, with one nuance. Rumelhart developed backpropagation independently in the spring of nineteen eighty two, and showed it to Terry Sejnowski, who found it trained much faster than Boltzmann machines. Hinton was not convinced at first and preferred Boltzmann machines. He accepted backprop about a year later. By nineteen eighty six, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Ronald Williams had the Nature paper that made it standard. So the rejection was real, but it lasted roughly a year, not a decade. And there is a nice irony here. Hinton's own Nobel lecture, delivered in December twenty twenty four, was titled Boltzmann machines.

The Nobel itself is confirmed. The Royal Swedish Academy split the twenty twenty four Physics prize between John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational discoveries that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks. There was a second AI Nobel that year too, in Chemistry, split between David Baker, Demis Hassabis, and John Jumper for computational protein design and protein structure prediction.

Now the technical claim, which is the real point of the video. The old worry was that a ball rolling downhill finds the nearest ditch, not the lowest point. The video's answer is that in a space with billions of dimensions, a true minimum would require every single direction to curve upward at once, which is vanishingly unlikely. What looks like a pit in a two dimensional slice is almost always a saddle. The Hessian, which is the table of second derivatives, encodes this. If all its eigenvalues are positive, you are in a bowl. If even one is negative, you are on a saddle. A saddle is not a trap, it is a delay. The slope there is faint, the descent crawls, then it finds the way down and falls away.

The video earns that with a striking visual. It plots loss on a random two dimensional slice of a real network with about one thousand three hundred weights, learning to separate two spirals. As training proceeds, the point barely moves across the slice. The ground beneath it sinks. The reason is that the gradient has one thousand three hundred components, and almost all of its length points out of the slice, in directions the picture cannot show. Every landscape diagram you have ever seen is a two dimensional shadow of something vastly larger. And shadows mislead.

One correction from the replies. A commenter says the tweet is misleading, because large language models do not have billions of dimensions. That is half right. The hidden width of a model is indeed only tens of thousands. But the loss landscape lives in parameter space, and a large model has billions of parameters, so the landscape really does have billions of dimensions. The comment mixes up the width of a layer with the dimension of the space the loss is defined over. The video makes the point correctly, and its illustration holds at small scale too.

The video also earns its history. Rosenblatt and the Perceptron in nineteen fifty eight, with its guarantee that if any straight line can separate the data, the Perceptron will find one. The X O R problem that breaks it, and Minsky and Papert's nineteen sixty nine book Perceptrons, which is often blamed for the long chill that followed. The cure, adding a hidden layer, which moves the points into new coordinates where one line is enough. The Universal Approximation Theorem from nineteen eighty nine, and its crucial omission. It promises the right weights exist, and says nothing about how to find them. Then the gradient descent loop, the learning rate, minibatches, momentum, the Adam optimizer, spelled A D A M, and the chain rule that backpropagation uses, plus the vanishing and exploding gradient problem, which is why careful initialization, the rectifier activation, spelled R E L U, and residual connections matter. Sepp Linnainmaa, spelled L I N N A I N M A A, described the reverse sweep in nineteen seventy, and Paul Werbos applied it to networks in his nineteen seventy four thesis.

Two more things the video gets right that are worth knowing. It separates optimization from generalization. Training loss keeps sinking while loss on data the network never saw turns back up, which is memorization of noise. And it knocks down the flat minima theory. You can rescale a network's weights so it computes exactly the same function while its valley looks as sharp as you like, so flatness depends on how you measure it. There is evidence that flatness helps, but it is not a law.

And it closes honestly. Low training loss is only half the job. We still cannot fully explain why the answers found this way work so well on things the network has never seen.

So the central claim is correct and well argued, the history is right, and the one criticism in the replies is a mix up. If you watch one thing this week, watch this one. It is the clearest explanation of gradient descent and backpropagation I have seen, and it ends on the actual open question in machine learning instead of pretending it is solved.
