His argument in one line: apply Bruce Yandle's nineteen eighty three theory of Bootleggers and Baptists to Dario Amodei, and the sermon about AI safety hides a bootlegger's interest in keeping the AI shop closed on Sundays.

Here is the framework. In nineteen eighty three the economist Bruce Yandle noticed that Sunday liquor bans had two kinds of backers. Preachers wanted the morals. Bootleggers wanted the business, because once the legal shops closed the trade was all theirs. His rule, as Leo restates it: skip the sermon, check whose store is closed on Sunday. I verified this. Wikipedia, Ballotpedia, and the American Enterprise Institute all date the concept to nineteen eighty three, and Yandle's original piece ran in Regulation magazine. So the theory and the date are accurate.

Now the four claims, one at a time.

Claim one, the preacher and the bootlegger are the same person. Leo says Amodei plays both roles: the public safety sermon and the private commercial pitch. His evidence is that Anthropic's own IPO paperwork contains both the warning that Claude must not be left to build the next model alone, and language describing one Claude speeding up the next as closing the loop. I could confirm the underlying tension is real, but I could not verify the specific quote closing the loop in any search, and the filing date is off. Anthropic's public S one was filed on October twenty sixth, twenty twenty six, and the prospectus is dated November seventeenth. I could not find a November second filing. So treat the quote as unconfirmed and the date as wrong.

Claim two, the charter gives control to a trust nobody elected. This one is solid and it is the strongest part of the post. Anthropic's prospectus creates five share classes. Class F is a single founder share carrying a fixed fifty point one percent of the vote with no economic rights. Class T is a single share held by the Long Term Benefit Trust, and it picks the board majority. Class A is what the public buys and it tops out at forty nine point nine percent no matter how many shares are bought. Motley Fool, Gate, and a November seventeenth prospectus walkthrough all describe exactly that structure. One more share, held by a trust nobody elected, picks the board majority. You can own the stock and you cannot outvote one share. That checks out.

Claim three, the certification regime would lock open weights out. David Sacks has said publicly that requiring pre-release safety certification creates a framework where closed models pass and open source models fail by default, because open models cannot undergo testing once their weights are released. Multiple outlets report this accusation and name Anthropic and OpenAI as the actors pushing it. The underlying mechanism is sound. One caveat worth holding: Sacks is an interested party here, and the certification regime he is warning about does not yet exist. It is a proposal under debate, not a rule in force.

Claim four, the money. This is where the post is best documented. Dario Amodei personally gave one million dollars to the super PAC Public First, reported by Politico in July. Anthropic contributed forty million dollars to Public First Action, per Bloomberg in September. Public First spent more than sixty three million dollars in the twenty twenty six midterms, and ten of the seventeen congressional candidates it endorsed won, per Bloomberg on November seventh. So the money trail checks out at every number Leo cites.

Two further links in that trail are real but need one correction. Brian Mast, the House Foreign Affairs chairman who led the letter to Jensen Huang and threatened a subpoena, did take money from Anthropic's own PAC. The PAC exists, it is registered with the Federal Election Commission as the Anthropic P B C Political Action Committee, and The Hill reported it gave about two hundred fifty seven thousand dollars to federal candidates and party committees between July and September, split almost evenly between Republicans and Democrats. And the Georgetown think tank is CSET, the Center for Security and Emerging Technology, which received fifty five million dollars in startup funding from Open Philanthropy, whose co-founder Holden Karnofsky later joined Anthropic. TechFlow published that investigation. So the funding web is documented.

Now the claim that gets the most attention and the least support: that this money caused Jensen Huang's ouster. Leo says the spy scandal has zero evidence, and that part is right. Forbes reported on November twenty fifth that U.S. intelligence officials told congressional staffers the United States has no evidence China directed Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive, to pursue the affair. Nvidia says the diversion allegations were thoroughly investigated and debunked last year. The intelligence community still does not know what the relationship was. Zero evidence is fair. But note what happened next, because Leo's post does not mention it: Nvidia's board reinstated Huang on November twenty third, and the subpoena threat is still live. And a money trail that runs from one side to a lawmaker is a conflict of interest. It is not the same thing as causation, and Yandle's own theory does not require anyone to conspire. The money shows up and everybody does their job. Leo argues that correctly. What the post does not establish is that the money changed how anyone voted or wrote a letter.

The bigger gap is that the post argues about who gets to run AI, and never actually answers whether Amodei's governance is good or bad for safety. Those are separable questions. The charter design is genuinely concentrated, and it is also genuinely conservative for safety: one unelected trust holding the board majority is a structural check on the company by people who do not profit from more AI. A skeptic can read the same structure as an independent safety council rather than a cartel. Both readings are consistent with the same share table.

My take. This is the sharpest governance critique of the Anthropic IPO I have seen, and the charter section is worth reading twice on its own. The money trail is genuinely documented, and so is the open-source structural argument. Two things are weak here. The first is the filing date and the closing the loop quote. The second is the causal leap from money to the Huang ouster, which is an inference about motives that Leo does not label as one. The post ends by returning to Huang's framing, that AI is so scary only they should do it. The interesting inversion is that Anthropic's own charter and safety posture is closer to the bootlegger side of Huang's framing than his public post suggests, and that is a real tension. Whether the trust that holds the board majority is a check or a cartel depends on who you think benefits from the market staying closed.

One last thing, for the audio. The quote he attributes to Jensen Huang, that AI is so scary only they should do it, I could not locate in Huang's own words. Treat that as Leo's paraphrase rather than a sourced quote. And the post was written while the author was on vacation, so expect it to be argued rather than sourced. That is its appeal and its limit.
