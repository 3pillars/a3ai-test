This is a post on X by Jen Zhu, posted today, about what stays valuable as AI automates the routine work. Let me verify the claims and give you the takeaways.

The setup. She quote tweets a short video from the account el cine, with the caption "A I taking over video editing." Her point is about opportunity cost. Once routine cuts, silence removal, and basic sync happen in minutes, every hour spent doing them by hand is an hour not spent on pacing, taste, or client judgment. And she says the same logic applies to any field being automated. Three questions to keep asking: what to automate, what to do with our time, and what remains as our premium.

First, what is actually in the video. It is twelve point eight seconds long and there is no narration at all, just music. What you see is Adobe Premiere Pro, with an auto cut panel and a script panel side by side. The script is Python style code calling Premiere's own scripting interface. You can read the calls: cut the sequence at twelve point four eight seconds, update the preview, save the project with an auto generated version name, add a caption to a clip, apply an effect to a clip, and pull the main sequence. So the demonstration is real. It is not an AI generating video. It is an agent scripting Premiere Pro to perform ordinary edit operations automatically.

That matters for the framing. The claim that routine cuts, silence removal, and basic sync now take minutes is accurate. Adobe ships text based editing in Premiere, where the timeline is edited through the transcript, and it detects pauses and filler words. There are open source agents that plug into Premiere through the U X P panel and a local server to remove silence, drop repeated takes, and add captions. The mechanical part of editing is genuinely collapsing to minutes.

Now the honest measurement. Her own post is not landing. Sixteen likes, four reposts, five replies, and about two thousand two hundred views, from an account with roughly sixty two thousand followers. The video she quote tweets has two hundred thirty thousand views, two thousand two hundred likes, two hundred thirty one reposts, and one hundred seventy four replies. The underlying demo gets roughly a hundred times the attention of the commentary. That tells you the argument is doing less work than the demo.

And the two replies in the thread are the sharpest part of the whole thing, and they are hers by topic if not by authorship. The first one says the catch is that most people never spend the saved hour on taste. The second says the same hours just get spent checking whether the output is right. Both are pointing at the same failure mode. Automation does not upgrade you. It hands you the hours, and the default outcome is that you spend them verifying the machine instead of doing the work only you can do. Which is exactly the risk in her own framing. If the saved hour is absorbed by checking, then the premium you were going to earn on taste and judgment never gets earned. The three questions she lists only work if the answer to the second one is not more checking.

My read. The thesis is right and it is not new, but it is the correct framing for where the labor market is heading, and she is a serious voice on it. She is the co founder and chief executive of Power Dynamics, the founder of the tech fellowship at the Aspen Institute, and a research fellow in philosophy of intelligence at Oxford. The asymmetry between the engagement on the post and the engagement on the demo is the more interesting signal than the post itself. The work is spreading faster than the thinking about the work.

If you are in the same spot, here is the practical version. Automate the part you can describe in a sentence, spend the freed hour on the part you cannot describe in a sentence, and pick a check that does not consume the saving. Video editing is a good test case because the mechanical layer, the cuts, the silence removal, the captions, the sync, is now cheap and fast, and the judgment layer, what to keep, where to pace it, what the client actually felt, is not automated at all.
