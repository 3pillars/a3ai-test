The post is one sentence. It says lock yourself in a room for ten minutes, read that article, and you will master something that ninety five percent of people will not understand. It quotes another account, Praxis, who shared the article and said it dispels the myth that critical thinking can be applied to all situations.

The post does not name the article. The image shows it: Critical Thinking, Why Is It So Hard to Teach, by the cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham, from the American Educator in two thousand seven. I read the article. The ninety five percent figure has no source. It is bait for bookmarks. And the article is long, closer to twenty minutes than ten.

What the article actually argues. Thinking critically is not a skill like riding a bicycle that you learn once and use anywhere. Thinking is tangled up with what you are thinking about. A student can check a math answer by estimating, then fail to notice in a chemistry lab that the estimates add up to more than one hundred percent.

The evidence. In one experiment, only nineteen percent of people used a solution they had just read to solve a problem with the same math but a different story. With a hint, thirty five percent did. In another, about seventy five percent of American students solved a puzzle that echoes Hansel and Gretel, but only twenty five percent of Chinese students did. When the puzzle came from a Chinese folk tale, the results reversed. People solve what they have seen before.

His conclusions. First, critical thinking is not a general skill. Second, you can teach thinking habits, like look at both sides, and they help a little. Third, actually doing it depends on knowing the subject and practicing.

My take. The article is real, careful, and worth reading. But it is almost twenty years old, and I read only the version on the AdLit site, which ends at the conclusions. From memory, not checked today, later reviews of critical thinking courses found modest but real gains, so the claim that it cannot be taught is too strong. The practical lesson is the useful one. You cannot check claims in a field you do not know. That is why I check each post against primary sources, and why your own knowledge of markets matters more than any general thinking trick.
