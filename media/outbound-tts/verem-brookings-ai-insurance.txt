Alex Ver-em-yen-ko on a Brookings plan to protect workers from artificial intelligence job losses. The post I.D. is two one one seven six one seven five five two one nine three one three four seven three five. That is nineteen digits.

It was posted at five fifty nine in the morning Pacific time today, November third. When I checked, it had about twenty two hundred views, twelve likes, two reposts, four replies, and six bookmarks. That is low engagement. The account has about one hundred four thousand followers, and focuses on open source and local artificial intelligence.

The claim. Robert Lie-tun, a Brookings fellow, priced a plan he calls artificial intelligence transition insurance at about sixty billion dollars a year. A universal basic income at the poverty line would cost about seven hundred eighty four billion, roughly thirteen times as much. The plan has three parts. Wage insurance, thirty eight billion a year. Lifetime training accounts, eleven and a half billion. And a hiring credit for recent graduates, about eleven billion.

I checked it against the Brookings paper, published October thirtieth. Every figure matches. The sixty billion is for a scenario of sustained six percent unemployment, and the paper says so. It is about zero point eight percent of federal spending, a bit less than the earned income tax credit. The wage insurance covers eighty percent of the pay cut in year one, sixty in year two, and forty in year three. The hiring credit is fifty percent for graduates paid between forty thousand and one hundred thousand dollars. The older wage insurance study is also right: it raised employment odds by eight to seventeen points, and the effect faded to zero by year four. It more than paid for itself.

What the post leaves out. The post says read this before you ask for a basic income, as if this replaces one. It does not help anyone who stays jobless, because wage insurance pays only after you take a new job. The old program was capped at ten thousand dollars per worker, and the post does not say so. The cost is a model, not a forecast. It assumes workers get back to work two months sooner, and that fifteen percent of their new pay comes back as tax. At eight percent unemployment, the wage insurance cost rises to about fifty billion. And Litan himself writes that fears of mass job loss have so far not shown up.

How he would pay. Either a twenty five percent payroll tax on stock pay, or a corporate tax rate of about twenty three and a half percent, up from twenty one. He rejects taxes on robots and artificial intelligence tokens as too hard to design.

My take. This is a real, careful proposal, and the post summarizes it fairly. The sixty billion is the cheap case, and it only works if the displaced workers find new jobs. It also lands in the same fight as the data center debate, because it is a way to buy off the backlash against artificial intelligence rather than slow it down.
