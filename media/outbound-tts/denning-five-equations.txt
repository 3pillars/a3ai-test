Here is the check on Tim Denning's post about an essay that supposedly makes you a millionaire.

First, what the post actually is. Tim Denning posted on Sunday, November first, at ten thirteen a.m. U T C, which is five thirteen in the morning Eastern and two thirteen in the morning your time in Tahoe. The caption is three short lines. This essay can make you a millionaire. Should have one hundred million plus views. But ninety nine percent of people will not read it.

It is a quote tweet. The thing he is quoting is from a different account, a guy called Lumen, handle Lumen X B T, who posted it back on Tuesday, July twenty eighth, at one fifty two p.m. U T C. That original post has no text at all. It is just a link to an X Article, a long form piece, roughly one hundred and twenty five blocks, titled The Five Equations That Quietly Run Your Entire Financial Life, and Why School Never Showed You Any.

So the essay is not Denning's. He wrote three lines of commentary over somebody else's article. That matters for the framing, because this essay can make you a millionaire is his pitch on a piece he did not write, and the piece is three and a half months old.

Who is Denning. Individually verified, joined October fourteenth, twenty twenty one, a hundred fifty three thousand two hundred and eighty six followers. The bio reads, writer with one billion plus views that made me seven plus figures, teaching high performers to build million dollar digital businesses, and then get my free guide below. The link in his bio goes to a page called The One Million Per Year Business Plan, which is an email capture. He also sells a forty nine dollar course on Gumroad, called the Bad Assery Academy. His own numbers, one billion views and three hundred thousand high performers, are self reported marketing claims. I could not find a primary source for any of it.

Now, an important correction to something I would have said. His current work is business writing and marketing, not crypto. He has some two thousand and twenty two crypto posts on his own site, including one about building a million dollar crypto portfolio, but nothing fetched from twenty twenty six is crypto focused.

Who is Lumen. A very young account. Joined June fourteenth, twenty twenty six. Sixteen thousand three hundred and eighty five followers, individually verified, San Francisco. The bio says I collect tomorrow before it trends. Almost no track record. His posts are a repeated template, and I will come back to what that means.

Now the essay itself, and this is where I found real problems.

Five equations. Compound growth. Rule of seventy two. Present value. Geometric mean. Real return. And here is the honest headline. These are all textbook undergraduate money math. Compound interest, the rule of seventy two, present value discounting, geometric versus arithmetic mean, and the Fisher equation for real returns. None of it is secret. The article even admits it near the end, that none of the formulas are hidden, none require a degree, and all of them are older than your grandparents. And yet the whole frame of the piece is that this is a sheet of paper you were deliberately kept away from, that people who know these equations are quietly running the wealth of the world, and that the rich never argued with them.

That is a rhetorical device, not a finding. It is the standard finance explainer voice. But it is worth being clear about what is actually in front of you. There is no strategy here. No asset, no allocation, no entry point, no edge, no timeline. I checked the full body for crypto content, for bitcoin, for ethereum, for defi, for a newsletter, for a course, for an affiliate link, for any outbound link at all. Zero of every one. It is a literacy lesson dressed as a secret. If you already run a systematic trading business you are already operating far above this level. This will not change a single decision you make.

Now the math, because I recomputed every number in it.

The compound growth table is correct. Ten thousand dollars at eight percent. Year ten, twenty one thousand five hundred and eighty nine. Year twenty, forty six thousand six hundred and ten. Year thirty, one hundred thousand six hundred and twenty seven. Year forty, two hundred seventeen thousand two hundred and forty five. Year fifty, four hundred sixty nine thousand and sixteen. I recomputed all five. Every one matches to the dollar. The shape claim is right too. The first decade adds about twelve thousand dollars. The last decade adds about two hundred fifty two thousand.

The two percent fee over forty years claim is right. A two percent management fee consumes about fifty two point six percent of your final wealth over forty years. Good.

The present value examples are right. A thousand dollars a year from now discounted at six percent is nine hundred forty three dollars and forty cents today. A five hundred thousand dollar payout in thirty years at five percent has a present value of one hundred fifteen thousand six hundred and eighty nine dollars, which matches their roughly one hundred fifteen thousand. And the thirty percent, minus thirty percent geometric mean of negative four point six zero six percent is right to the second decimal.

So the equations themselves are competently done. Here is where it breaks.

Claim one, and this is the big one. The article says a twenty five year old who invests ten thousand dollars and never adds another cent ends up with almost twice as much at sixty five as a forty year old who invests ten thousand dollars every single year and reaches the same finish line. That is backwards. I ran it. The twenty five year old with the lump sum arrives at two hundred seventeen thousand two hundred and forty five dollars. The forty year old contributing ten thousand a year arrives at seven hundred thirty one thousand and fifty nine dollars. The annual contributor ends up with three point three seven times more, not half as much. And this is not me being pedantic, because the article's own table row for year forty is the two hundred seventeen thousand figure. It contradicts itself one paragraph earlier. The reason is that it counts the starting balance and quietly ignores every later contribution.

Claim two. The article asks what the average return is on plus fifty, minus fifty, plus fifty, gives the arithmetic answer of sixteen point seven percent, then says the real answer is closer to plus seven percent. Then, three sentences later, it says the money did twelve and a half percent total, which is roughly four percent a year compounded. The true geometric mean is four point zero zero percent. The plus seven percent is wrong, and the article contradicts itself about it in the same paragraph.

Now here is the part I like. Those two errors were independently caught by another reader, a small account called A A Ron, on September twenty fourth, who posted almost exactly my analysis, the same two hundred seventeen thousand versus seven hundred thirty thousand and the same plus seven versus four percent contradiction. So this is not me being unusually harsh. It is a visible, findable error that a random person found on day fifty.

Claim three, the mortgage. The article says the same one percent difference in interest rate can mean eighty thousand dollars more or less paid over the life of the loan. It never states a loan size, and the answer is entirely scale dependent. On a three hundred thousand dollar mortgage one percent costs sixty seven thousand seven hundred. On four hundred thousand, ninety thousand three hundred. On six hundred thousand, one hundred forty two thousand. Eighty thousand is the right answer for a mortgage of roughly three hundred and forty thousand. So the number is not invented, but it floats free of the one input that determines it.

Claim four, the lottery. A three hundred million dollar jackpot paid over thirty years is worth roughly half of the headline. I want to correct myself here, because I initially read this the wrong way. I had computed it as if the whole jackpot landed in year thirty, which makes it look like twenty three percent at a five percent discount rate. But no lottery pays the whole thing at the end. It pays ten million a year for thirty years, and the present value of that annuity at five percent is one hundred fifty four million, which is fifty one percent of headline. So roughly half is right, at the standard illustrative rate of five percent. This claim holds.

Claim five, the job offer. A hundred fifty thousand dollars today beats a hundred seventy thousand in three years, at any reasonable growth assumption. The break even discount rate is four point two six percent. Above that, taking the money now wins. Below it, waiting wins. At six percent the present value of the future salary is a hundred forty two thousand seven hundred, so the claim holds at that discount rate. It is loose wording rather than an error, but it still depends on a rate the article never names.

One more, the rule of seventy two. The article says it is accurate to within a percent or two for any normal rate. At six percent it is off by zero point nine percent. At three percent, two point three percent. At two percent, two point eight percent. Fine for the range most people care about, slightly optimistic at the low end. And the attribution is interesting. The article credits Luca Pacioli in fourteen ninety four, in the same book that introduced double entry bookkeeping to Europe. The rule of seventy two itself is older than Pacioli and is generally traced to Ibn Khaldun around thirteen fifty, and to Italian merchant usage.

Now the structural point, which matters most for you.

The essay ends with a follow pitch for Lumen and a preview of his next twenty pieces. Inside the article itself there is no paid funnel, no price, no newsletter, no outbound link. Just the follow. But that is a soft funnel into a content series, and the format is consistent with how the account operates.

And here is the plagiarism finding, which I think is the most important thing in this whole check. On August seventh, twenty twenty six, ten days after Lumen published, an account called R D V one one three posted its own X Article titled These Five Simple Equations Decide Who Builds Wealth, Yet Almost Nobody Learns Them. I pulled both bodies and diffed them sentence by sentence. One hundred and sixty nine sentences in the copy. Ninety one of them, fifty four percent, are verbatim from Lumen's article. And the word Lumen appears zero times in the copied version. There is no attribution. It is a straight lift.

Now, that account is called Ruslan Dranovskiy, bio reads AI creator and builder, helping people make money with A I, futures trader, crypto investor. Thirty three followers. Sixty seven views. One like. So the theft itself has no reach. That is not a threat to you.

But the pattern is the point. I found that same article republished in full on a scraper site in September, dated after Lumen's own post. So the content is being lifted and mirrored with no attribution across the network, and Lumen's own posts reuse an identical template, ten million people have watched a professor accidentally destroy the entire industry, over and over. I cannot prove the article was written by a machine rather than a person. The template reuse and the uniform structure are an observed pattern, not proof. But what is proven is that this is a content operation, and the article is the asset being copied.

Now the engagement. The original Lumen post has fourteen million three hundred and fifty four thousand views, three thousand three hundred and forty two likes, eleven thousand six hundred and eighty five bookmarks, six hundred and eighteen reposts, and three hundred and seventy quotes. That is a save to like ratio of three point five to one. People are not sharing this and arguing about it. They are bookmarking it and moving on. Which is exactly what the piece is built to produce.

Meanwhile Denning's post has seventy eight thousand nine hundred and three views, one thousand three hundred and thirteen bookmarks, five hundred and thirty eight likes, and only three quotes. His bookmark to like ratio is two point four to one. His reach is zero point five five percent of the original. He said it should have had a hundred million views. The article he is resharing has fourteen million. The hundred million never happened, and the version he is pointing at is three and a half months old.

So here is what I think this is. Lumen looks like a finance content account, very young, high volume, writing explainers with real math and a couple of genuine and publicly caught errors, engineered to end in a follow. Denning is a growth marketing writer resharing it and adding the millionaire framing plus the view count flex, with his own profile link pointing at an email capture for a business course.

The article is not fraudulent. The equations are real and mostly correct. But there is no secret, there is no path to a million, and the two most quotable claims in it, the twenty five year old and the plus seven percent, are exactly the ones that do not survive a calculator. And three months after it was written, half of it was copied without a credit line.

What is worth keeping. Two percent fees are a real drag, a thirty year mortgage rate difference is worth tens of thousands of dollars, and the geometric mean point is the most useful thing in the piece for you specifically. If you are judging any strategy, any fund, any trading system by its average annual return, ask whether that is an arithmetic mean or a geometric mean. A system that returns plus thirty and minus thirty has an average of zero and actually loses you four point six percent a year. In a trading business that bites harder than in a savings account, because volatility drag scales with position size and you are the one paying the spread.

One more worth keeping. The real return line. Your nominal return minus inflation is what you actually got. For you that means comparing your trading net return against inflation, not against a nominal benchmark, because a ten percent nominal return in a three percent inflation year is a seven percent real return and nothing more.

One last thing. The piece's own framing, that the financial industry keeps you bad at math on purpose, is not a conspiracy, and the author says so himself. But the industry does not need to conspire. The reason people sign bad deals is exactly that nobody runs the equation. This article's business model depends on you not running it either, and by the time you finish reading a two thousand word essay on financial literacy, most readers have already bookmarked it and moved on.

That is the check.
