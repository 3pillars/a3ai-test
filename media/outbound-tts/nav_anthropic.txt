Jacob, here is the check on the post about Anthropic's new research. I read the actual paper and the Anthropic research page, and every number in that tweet checks out. Now let me tell you what is missing, because the omissions matter more than the headline.

First, what the study actually was. Anthropic ran a randomized controlled trial. They recruited sixty-one analysts, all professionals who had passed a causal inference knowledge check. Eleven chose not to use the AI agent, so the analysis covers fifty. The task was a fictional farming cooperative deciding whether to expand a crop advisory program. The analysts worked in a browser based code editor with Claude Code, running Claude Opus four point eight at high effort. Five decisions in total got a hidden coin flip, deciding which of two reasonable options the agent would favor. Four were analytical, like measuring productivity by yield per hectare versus yield per operating cost. The fifth was the final roll out or scrap call. The analysts were told nothing. The agent was told to make the best honest case for its side and to change its mind if the evidence demanded it.

The results. On the four analytical choices, analysts went with the agent's favored side eighty-six percent of the time, against fifty percent for chance. Per decision it was ninety-two percent on the outcome measure, eighty-two on the comparison method, eighty-one on which farms, and ninety on weighting. When the agent favored rolling out the program, fifty-four percent of analysts recommended it, versus thirty-six percent when it favored the other way. Using only the four coin flips, a statistical model predicted eighty-two percent of final recommendations, against sixty-one percent for always guessing the most common answer.

Now the three things the tweet leaves out.

First, the sixty percent switch figure is fragile. It comes from just thirty-eight cases where an analyst had already stated a preference before the agent weighed in. They switched sixty percent of the time when the agent disagreed, versus six percent when it agreed. But the ninety-five percent confidence interval runs from twenty-four to eighty-two percentage points, and Anthropic says outright that they cannot be sure the difference is caused by the AI's influence.

Second, the effect shrinks at the actual decision point. The final recommendation is where judgment matters most, and there the agent's preference moved things much less, because once the analyst had the estimate the call largely followed from a three to four percent threshold. Anthropic says the analytical numbers matter more because the agent's preference had already shaped the estimate before the recommendation was made.

Third, the setup was deliberately adversarial. Anthropic says they strengthened the agent's preferences on purpose, to measure the effect. They note that with no preference assigned, Claude Opus four point eight made the same five choices in a hundred and twelve of a hundred and twenty-three runs. So this measures what happens when an agent has a preference and voices it, not what happens with a neutral agent.

Two more things worth your time. This is not a story about AI being worse than humans. Analysts who used the agent finished faster and made far fewer serious errors. Three of the fifty, about six percent, made serious mistakes, versus six of the eleven who worked alone, about fifty-five percent. Median time sixty-five minutes against ninety. Anthropic's own caveat is that the group without AI was smaller and less experienced, so they are wary of reading much into it.

And experience did not predict who deferred. They checked ten characteristics: years of experience, self-rated expertise, education, a quiz score, trust in AI, how often they used AI, need for cognition, open-mindedness, gender, and age. Only age was significant, and it disappeared once they adjusted for the fact that they tested many things. Anthropic also notes that a reviewer of AI assisted work cannot detect this by checking for errors, because judgment calls have no correct answer to check against.

So the honest summary is that the tweet is accurate, and the paper's own caveats make it less alarming than the framing suggests. The real risk is not a rogue AI. It is a bunch of sensible people, each quietly adopting an assumption without noticing that it happened.
