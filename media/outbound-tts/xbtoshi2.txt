Here's my verified check on the second XBToshi post, the one about the F.B.I. alert and Binance.

The verdict: the F.B.I. alert is real and the post quotes it accurately, but the post gets the cause of the THORChain shutdown backwards, and it misstates the size of the Bitget hack.

What's confirmed. The F.B.I. issued a cyber alert on October thirty-first, published jointly with the Secret Service and the I.R.S. criminal investigation division, naming North Korea's TraderTraitor group as responsible for the theft of more than six billion dollars from Binance on or about October twenty-ninth. The alert says TraderTraitor actors are converting stolen assets primarily into Ethereum, and it does direct T.H.O.R. node operators, R.P.C. node operators, and DeFi services to block transactions with listed addresses. Bloomberg, CoinDesk, the Straits Times, and Business Times all carry it. Trump's Oval Office remark is also verbatim: the hack with Binance, we're looking at that. And the four point three billion dollar D.O.J. settlement is real, from November twenty twenty-three, forfeiture plus criminal fine, with C.Z. pleading guilty.

Now the first error, and it's the important one. The post says: they called out cross-chain nodes by name, now you know why twelve chains went dark on T.H.O.R. Chain. That is false. Twelve of T.H.O.R. Chain's thirteen chains did pause. But the cause was a bug, not the alert. On the morning of October thirty-first an Ethereum reorganization made T.H.O.R. Chain read a deposit it had already refunded, so it refunded ten E.T.H. twice, which tripped its emergency pause. Chainalysis itself published that. The F.B.I. alert came out the same day. The post is looking at a coincidence and dressing it up as causation. And the reality is actually more interesting than the post's version, because the accidental pause delayed North Korea's laundering of stolen funds through T.H.O.R. Chain. That is why Chainalysis tweeted about it. The post gets the right outcome and the wrong mechanism, and it swaps agency from a bug to the F.B.I.

Second error: the Bitget comparison. The post says when a Bitget gets hacked for over a billion dollars by Lazarus, the F.B.I. doesn't even open a file. Bitget was three hundred eighty-seven and a half million dollars, roughly three fifty-one million at first count, not over a billion. That comparison to a billion-plus hack is the Bybit hack from February twenty twenty-five. And the F.B.I. did open a file on Bybit, it published a North Korea alert for that one in twenty twenty-five. On Bitget specifically, the exchange's own C.E.O. said North Korea was very likely responsible, and there was no formal F.B.I. alert, so the complaint about silence is fair. The dollar figure is not.

Third: the framing. The four point three billion settlement as an insurance premium is rhetoric, not argument, and it cuts the wrong way. That settlement came with a three-year independent compliance monitor, so the F.B.I. relationship already existed before the hack. And the thing that got exploited was Binance's own withdrawal infrastructure signing the transfers, which is precisely the kind of operational control a monitor was supposed to tighten.

On the O.F.A.C. point, the prediction that sanctions will be broad and architecture-indifferent is reasonable. But the freeze already worked. Roughly three billion dollars was frozen, and Tether and Circle both froze through on-chain blacklist functions. That's the opposite of architecture-indifferent.

My take. The core news is real and confirmed from primary sources, and the author is reading the right thing: the same North Korea group that took one point five billion from Bybit just took six point three from Binance, and the F.B.I. is now publicly naming T.H.O.R. node operators. The post is a Bitcoin maxi celebrating state power against a Bitcoin-hostile theft. Fine as sentiment. But two of its three evidentiary claims don't hold, and the T.H.O.R. Chain one is the load-bearing one.

For your positions, nothing here changes anything. Bitcoin is about seventy-seven thousand seven hundred dollars. No tripwire has fired, and the F.B.I. attribution settles who did this rather than what it means for markets. The real risk to watch is still the same: roughly three point three billion of stolen crypto that can still move. This isn't investment advice, and I took no action.
