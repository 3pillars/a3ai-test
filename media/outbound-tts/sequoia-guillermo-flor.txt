Quick check on Guillermo Flor's X thread about Sequoia's A I Ascent.

The post I D is two one one seven three nine six three four one five six seven four four three one two nine. That is nineteen digits. It went up Monday, November second, at three twenty in the afternoon Pacific time. As of my check it has three hundred twenty views, four likes, no reposts, two replies and eight bookmarks. That is a small post from an account with about thirteen thousand followers.

The verdict. The thread is a faithful summary of Sequoia's keynote, with one framing problem. It says Sequoia just hosted the event. It did not. The event was April twentieth, twenty twenty-six, and Sequoia published its own recap on May eighth. So this is a six month old keynote presented as fresh.

What checks out against Sequoia's recap and the keynote summaries. More than one hundred fifty founders and researchers attended, including Demis Hassabiss, Andray Karpathy, Greg Brockman, and Boris Cherny of Anthropic. The post says one hundred fifty, Sequoia says more than one hundred fifty. Pat Grady framed A I as a revolution in computation: not faster horses, but cars. During the first fifteen years of cloud, the software market grew from about three hundred fifty billion dollars to about six hundred fifty billion. Sequoia puts the services market that machines can now address at roughly ten trillion dollars. Early A I apps gave ten to forty percent productivity gains; the next generation is pitched at ten to forty times. The practical definition of A G I is a system that can be dispatched to do a job, recover from failure, and keep going until it is done. The founder advice is moats, affordance and diffusion. And Sonya Huang called twenty twenty-six the year of agents.

What to discount. The ten trillion dollar figure is a rough Sequoia estimate, and Sequoia itself said it could be five trillion or fifty trillion. It is the size of the services spend, not revenue that A I companies will capture. It also comes from a venture firm that invests in exactly this story. The ten to forty times claim is a framing, not a measurement. And the line that A I companies reach scale faster than cloud or mobile is Sequoia's assertion; I did not check it against revenue data. I read the first post and its twelve points. I did not open the full newsletter breakdown that it links to.

Takeaway. This is a decent compact version of the Sequoia thesis: the prize is selling finished work, not software seats. Treat the numbers as pitch material, and treat the freshness as misleading.
