While verifying, I found a story in the Zero Hedge sidebar that is directly relevant to your credit tripwire, and it is more important than the plane.

The headline is "Oh, By The Way, Private Credit Defaults Just Hit A Record." The numbers are from Fitch Ratings' third quarter private credit and middle market performance monitor, dated the twenty-ninth of October. The headline figure checks out. Fitch's private credit default rate reached six point three percent over the trailing twelve months ended in the third quarter of twenty twenty-six. That is the highest on record, up from six point zero percent in the second quarter. So the headline number is real, and I confirmed it directly against Fitch's own release, not just through the press.

Now the correction. ZeroHedge's summary of this story gets one number wrong, and it is the wrong number in a way that matters.

ZeroHedge wrote that Fitch recorded twenty-seven default events in the third quarter, compared with twenty in the second quarter. That is a misreading of Fitch's release. Fitch actually says the opposite. The twenty-seven default events came from twenty new unique defaulters in the third quarter, and the trailing twelve month total of unique defaulters rose to ninety-one, up from eighty-four in the second quarter. So the "twenty" is not the second quarter total. The second quarter total was eighty-four. ZeroHedge swapped a quarterly new-defaulter count for a trailing total.

Now here is the figure ZeroHedge left out, and it is the most important one in the release. When you count only hard outcomes, meaning bankruptcies, liquidations, and out-of-court restructurings, the default rate was zero point three percent in the third quarter, and it fell from zero point four percent in the second quarter. So the headline rate is at a record because of deferrals and extensions. The hard default rate is going the other way.

The breakdown. Over the trailing twelve months, interest deferrals and payment in kind accounted for fifty percent of default events. Stressed maturity extensions were thirty-eight percent. Uncured payment defaults were eight percent. Bankruptcies, liquidations, and out-of-court restructurings were four percent. So ZeroHedge's claim that roughly eighty-eight percent of defaults were restructurings rather than outright bankruptcy is correct. Fifty plus thirty-eight equals eighty-eight.

There is also a composition shift that nobody mentioned. The increase was driven entirely by Fitch's model-based credit opinion portfolio, which feeds middle market collateralized loan obligations. That rate rose to a record five point seven percent from four point nine percent, the eighth consecutive monthly increase. The privately monitored rating portfolio, used mostly by insurers for regulatory capital, actually declined, to eight point zero percent from nine point four percent.
