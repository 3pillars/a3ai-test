The post that carries it is from Watcher Guru, an aggregator account, with about four hundred twenty four thousand views and about four point three thousand likes. Its line, that the U.S. blocks China from remotely accessing advanced A I chips, is stronger than the rule.

Bottom line. The rule is real, and the summary is mostly right. Two details are off, and the headline overstates it.

What is confirmed. The Bureau of Industry and Security, part of the Commerce Department, published an interim final rule on Monday, November sixteenth. Its own press release says it was written by and at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council, as the summary says. The release says the aim is to stop remote computing arrangements in third countries from getting around existing chip restrictions.

What the rule does, per TechCrunch's reading of the text. It requires a license to ship advanced chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if they may provide compute to companies in China or other restricted countries. It applies at about twelve million units of the government's processing power metric, which TechCrunch says equals roughly seven hundred fifty H one hundred or H two hundred chips. It also covers indirect access through brokers. Existing data center operators have ninety days after the rule takes effect to get a license. It adds license requirements for chip exports to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, because Commerce says they carry a higher than normal risk of diversion. Those do not apply to chips destined for U.S. based companies. And it rescinds the Biden era diffusion rule.

Where the summary is off. First, it says the rule was issued November fifteenth. The agency's release is dated the sixteenth. Second, it says the rule requires licenses for remote access from data centers in those five countries. The rule actually licenses the export of chips and servers to data centers that could serve restricted customers, and separately adds those five countries to chip licensing. Third, the Watcher Guru headline says it blocks China. It is a licensing regime with a ninety day grace period, not a ban. I read the press release and TechCrunch, not the full rule text.

Context. This follows Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang's resignation days ago, which TechCrunch ties to a spy scandal involving a customer, Megaspeed. Reuters reported last week that Chinese A I firms were rushing to secure overseas capacity.
