## Notes on each

**1. Apollo.** The 400,000 figure is the standard peak (1966–67) Apollo program workforce including contractors. It appears in NASA's own program history and Wikipedia's Apollo program article (cite 142). Wikipedia's version: "At its peak, it employed over 400,000 employees and contractors around the country." NASA "Managing NASA in the Apollo Era" (SP-4102) is the underlying document.

**2. Empire State Building.** Sage's Encyclopedia of Urban History entry states construction required "seven million hours of labor, 200,000 cubic feet of stone, 6,500 windows, 10 million bricks". The figure circulates as a CNN statistic. It is well-attributed to a standard reference work rather than to the original 1931 construction records, so it is a published figure but not an audited primary record.

**3. Swanson's law.** "Solar module prices tend to drop 20 percent for every doubling of cumulative shipped volume." Wikipedia's Swanson's law and Experience curve effect articles both carry this. Shapiro's phrasing "every time the world doubles its solar capacity" is a fair paraphrase (the law is stated in cumulative shipped volume, which is what capacity tracks).

**4. $130B data centers.** Primary source is Data Center Watch's Q1 2026 report: "Q1 2026 produced the largest single-quarter concentration of blocked and delayed data center projects on record, with at least 75 projects worth approximately $130 billion disrupted by local opposition." Widely reported (Ars Technica, Fortune, Forbes, Newsweek). Caveat: Data Center Watch is an advocacy organization tracking opposition, not a government statistical agency; its counting methodology is not published in the video. Ars Technica noted the Q1 2026 total nearly matched the full-year 2025 total (~$156B).

**5. Pew AI sentiment.** Pew Research Center (Aug 2026): 52% of US adults are more concerned than excited about AI's increased use in daily life, up from 37% in 2021 and 50% in 2025. Only 9% are more excited than concerned. "About half" and "fewer than one in 10" are accurate.

**6. Job loss poll — REWORDED.** Pew's actual wording: "71% of adults think AI will lead to fewer jobs in the United States over the next two decades, up from 64% in 2024." The number is right; "leave the country with fewer jobs" is not what was asked. The question was about fewer jobs in the US, not workforce leaving the country.

**7. Engels' pause.** From Robert C. Allen, "Engels' pause: Technical change, capital accumulation, and inequality in the British industrial revolution," Research in Economics 63(2), 2009. Exact text: "According to the Crafts-Harley estimates of British GDP, output per worker rose by 46% between 1780 and 1840. Over the same period, Feinstein's real wage index rose by only 12%." Allen coined the term "Engels' pause" for this. Shapiro's numbers are exact. Note: some secondary summaries write the 46% as per capita GDP rather than output per worker; the paper itself says output per worker.

**8. Labor share.** BLS nonfarm business labor share, series begins Q1 1947. I pulled the full chart table (318 quarterly observations). 1947–1975 range: min 61.3, max 66.2. Latest: Q1 2026 = 53.6, Q2 2026 = 52.8. BLS: "the lowest level ever recorded." So "60 to 66 cents for half a century after WWII" is accurate (actually 61–66), and "under 53 cents, the lowest ever recorded" is exact. This is the strongest statistic in the video.

**9. Stock ownership.** Fed Distributional Financial Accounts, 2026 Q2: top 10% hold 88.1% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares; bottom 50% hold 0.6% (FRED series WFRBSB50203, latest 2026-04-01 = 0.6). Also for context: top 1% equity share 50.9% in Q2 2026, up from 42.8% in Q3 1989.

**10. Alaska PFD.** First dividend paid 1982. The original statute paid long-time residents more and was struck down by the US Supreme Court (U.S. v. Alaska Permanent Fund Trust). The per-capita version started 1983 and has been paid every year since. Shapiro's "every year since 1982" is accurate.

## What the video does NOT do
- Names no bill, statute, or funded mechanism for the proposed national fund. The thesis is a policy argument, not a proposal with legs.
- Names no economist besides Engels' pause. The closest primary work: Irving Fisher's "The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions" (1933), which proposed taxing government-created money at 100% to end the business cycle — a different argument (money-creation constraint, not ownership distribution). John Hickins' "The Nostalgia for the Future" (2000) makes the universal-ownership-of-the-economy argument explicitly.
- Does not mention UBI pilot evidence (Finland Kela, Kenya GiveDirectly, US Stockton), which is the only empirical evidence base for the universal-stake claim.

## The internal tension
The video's own cited statistics argue against its thesis. Engels' pause (46% output, 12% wages) and a labor share at its lowest on record (52.8%) and 88% equity concentration are all evidence that productivity gains flowed to capital owners rather than spreading. Shapiro treats ownership concentration as the thing to solve with universal stakes; the data show that when labor stopped being the bottleneck, gains did not automatically diffuse. The ownership-stakes proposal is a policy choice, not a conclusion from the evidence.
